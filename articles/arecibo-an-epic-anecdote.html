ARECIBO: An Epic Anecdote

For more than fifty years, a dish in a Puerto Rican valley listened to the sky, and even spoke back. The story of Arecibo.

AK
Adarsh Kumar
BS-MS 2019
- min read
In this article

    We Homo sapiens have two eyes, with which we gaze at the heavens and perceive tiny sources of glare. We have a third eye too, one that has helped us contemplate realms beyond sight: the radio telescope. This is the story of one of the greatest of them, Arecibo.

    Arecibo at a glance

    Location
    Arecibo, Puerto Rico
    Built
    1963, 305 m dish
    1974 message
    1,679 bits, aimed at a cluster 25,000 light-years away
    Collapse

    Archived data

    An insight

    In Arecibo, Puerto Rico, we find the Arecibo Observatory, owned by the National Science Foundation of the US. Built in 1963, it was home to one of the world's biggest single-unit radio telescopes until FAST in China began observations in 2016. It was a 305-metre spherical reflector made of perforated aluminium panels, with movable antenna structures positioned about 168 metres above the reflector's surface. The panels focused incoming radio waves onto the antennas, from where they were sent to the central processing unit.

    The observatory also included an auxiliary 30-metre telescope that served as a radio interferometer and as a means to study the Earth's atmosphere. One of the key features of the antenna structure was that it could be moved in any direction, which made it possible to track a particular celestial object across different parts of the sky and focus on it for long periods to gather valuable information.

    Major contributions

    Having such a huge dish to collect radio waves allowed Arecibo to see objects almost hidden from ordinary vision, which made it great at detecting objects like pulsars. Arecibo was one of the very few facilities that earned the title of “king” of pulsar hunting among scientists.

    In 1968, Arecibo observed the rapid spinning motion at the central region of the Crab Nebula, faster than even white dwarfs, which gave scientists a clearer picture of what pulsars actually are. Fourteen years later, Arecibo clocked a pulsar spinning every 1.6 milliseconds, unseating the Crab Pulsar as the fastest known pulsar.

    Observing rapid spins feels excellent, but Arecibo did not stop there. Observations in the early 1990s hinted that ice lurked in the permanently shadowed craters of Mercury (Fig. 1), which was later confirmed by NASA's MESSENGER spacecraft. Radio telescopes like FAST and the Green Bank Telescope are not equipped with radio transmitters, which made Arecibo an exception. Arecibo's transmitters allowed it to bounce radio waves off the surfaces and atmospheres of asteroids and planets, revealing crucial information such as shape, size and orbital track.

    This feature allowed Arecibo's radar vision, in the 1970s, to get the first large-scale view of Venus's surface and to map its terrain (Fig. 2). Five years before that, Arecibo's radar measurements revealed that Mercury spins on its axis once every 59 days, rather than 88 days. This cleared up a long-standing mystery about Mercury's temperature shifts, since 88 days is the time Mercury takes to orbit the Sun once.

    A message to the stars

    Arecibo was also the first facility to broadcast a radio message intended for an alien audience, in 1974. The message consisted of 1,679 bits of information, beamed towards a cluster of stars roughly 25,000 light-years away. The binary string consisted of the chemical formulas for components of DNA, a schematic of the solar system, a stick-figure sketch of a human, and some other scientific data.

    The recent decommission of an epic saga

    In August 2020, a cable holding up the central platform snapped, making a hole in the reflector dish. Three months later, a second cable broke, after which the National Science Foundation declared that the snapped cables were beyond repair and that the whole structure was in danger of collapsing. Days after the NSF's announcement, the remaining cables, weighing about 900 tonnes, broke and collapsed into the dish below, smashing it.

    Before the collapse, experts had indicated that the condition of the structure was alarming. The NSF evaluated multiple assessments by independent engineering companies and found that the remaining cables could no longer carry the loads they were designed to support.

    A potential replacement?

    There have been some plans for a replacement of the Arecibo telescope, but they are still in their early phases. Many proposals have been submitted to the National Science Foundation, which call for replacing the giant dish with an array of 1,000 small dishes, each 9 metres across, on the platform spanning the current dish.

    Regardless of its tenure, the contributions made by Arecibo have carried us far into the future. The data from the collapsed telescope has been safely moved and preserved at the Texas Advanced Computing Center, where over 3 petabytes of data from around 50 years of Arecibo sky scans have been transferred, so that students and scientists around the world can recall the sensational journey of Arecibo.

    Key takeaways

      What to remember

    • Arecibo's 305 m dish was one of the largest single radio telescopes for over fifty years.
    • It studied pulsars, found evidence of ice on Mercury, revealed Mercury's true spin, and mapped Venus by radar.
    • Unlike many radio telescopes, it could also transmit, and sent the 1974 message to the stars.
    • The telescope collapsed in late 2020, and its data was preserved at the Texas Advanced Computing Center.
