Radio Galaxies, Part 1: Fantastic Bursts and How to Find Them

From a Bell Labs hiss in the 1930s to arrays that span continents: how radio galaxies were found, and how astronomers see them.

DS
Devang Somawanshi
BS-MS 2020
- min read
In this article

    Well, readers, when we try to think closely about this word, the first idea we get is that there is a galaxy somewhere in the universe that is emitting radio waves. This notion is partially correct. You might be wondering why it is only partially correct. Hold that thought first; we will try to understand the history behind their discovery.

    Radio galaxies at a glance

    First detected
    Karl Jansky, 1933
    First radio map
    Grote Reber, 1940
    Emission type
    Polarized synchrotron radiation
    Resolution tricks
    Interferometry, aperture synthesis, lunar occultation
    Largest single dish
    FAST, China, 500 m

    The first radio maps

    Many people believe that the study of extraterrestrial radio waves started after World War II, when we began to point our radars from detecting enemy aircraft to the interstellar sky. That is not the case. Karl Jansky, who worked at Bell Telephone Laboratories, studied these extraterrestrial radio waves during the early 1930s. In 1933, he concluded that the radio waves he received at 15 m wavelengths were of extraterrestrial origin. He even found that most of the intensity came from the centre of the Milky Way. In 1940, Grote Reber drew the first radio map of the Milky Way. Grote even found two new regions in the night sky, Cassiopeia and Cygnus, from where he received intense radio emission.

    Discrete radio sources

    In 1946, J. S. Hey, S. J. Parsons and J. W. Phillips detected more discrete radio sources. They used the Yagi antenna, which had a beamwidth of 6 by 15. With this, they were able to draw a contour map. So what is a contour map? A map made up of contour lines. Suppose these lines are a function of two variables; the curve that unites points of equal value under the function is a contour line. If you draw this curve, you will see that it joins points with equal values, and such lines make up a contour map.

    Coming back to the map drawn by our three gentlemen: they identified two regions of intense radio emission, one at the galactic centre and the other in the constellation Cygnus. Hey and others were also able to find radio fluctuations from Cygnus. They concluded that this radio source must be small (tiny, meaning star-like), and such objects are called discrete radio sources.

    The English and Australians were pioneers in the discovery of these radio objects. J. L. Pawsey and his coworkers mounted an aerial system on a cliff at a seashore and used the sea surface as their reflector for a Lloyd's mirror interferometer. Martin Ryle and Graham Smith (not the cricketer) of England used a radio equivalent of Michelson's optical interferometer. With these instruments, they calculated the angular width of the radio source in terms of the wavelength λ and the distance d between the aerial systems, using the simple formula:

    θ = λ / d

    Two teams created ground-breaking radio telescope devices and used them to precisely locate and image faint radio sources. One was headed by Martin Ryle. The other was led by John Bolton, which determined that discrete radio sources were galaxies or supernova remnants rather than stars. Both studied Cygnus A, and they set an upper limit of 8 arc for its angular width.

    In 1948, Bolton did cutting-edge work by doing a radio survey of the night sky using a sea interferometer. He discovered at least six radio sources, including Taurus A, Coma A, Hercules A and Centaurus A. Radio astronomers in England and Australia raised the number of observed radio sources to thousands. Russia, the U.S.A., Canada, the Netherlands, Japan and India established essential research centres, which increased the number of discovered sources further.

    These discoveries are fascinating, but it would add to our fascination if we knew their nature. This can be done by optical identification and distance estimation. In this article, we mainly focus on distance estimation. Whatever comes next might sound bizarre, but many radio astronomers at that time were confused about whether these radio objects were present in our solar system or beyond it. This idea was finally struck down by Ryle and Smith. They showed that radio sources like Cygnus A and Cassiopeia A had to lie beyond the solar system. Bolton and his co-workers identified Taurus A (the Crab Nebula), Virgo A and Centaurus A with the Crab Nebula and the external galaxies NGC 4486 and NGC 5128, which put down the idea of these radio objects being in the solar system.

    During the 1950s, when Rock & Roll and classic pop music were on the rise, Walter Baade and Rudolph Minkowski made the optical identification of these radio sources and found Cygnus A's accurate location. Baade and Minkowski also detected more radio sources optically, such as Puppis A and NGC 1275.

    Can we classify them?

    The answer is yes. J. H. Oort, G. Westerbout and their contemporary B. Y. Mills came up with a classification system that put these radio objects into two groups: galactic radio sources and extragalactic radio sources. Baade and Minkowski divided each category into two more types.

    • Galactic: (a) supernova remnants such as Taurus A and Puppis A, and (b) hot H2 regions (massive molecular clouds responsible for star formation).
    • Extragalactic: (a) normal galaxies like M31 and M101, and (b) strong radio sources, primarily in elliptical galaxies. NGC 5128 and NGC 1316 come under this category.

    Origins

    Why are these galaxies called radio galaxies? Strong radio sources emit more radio energy than normal galaxies. You could say that the energy emitted by these sources at radio wavelengths is more than at optical wavelengths, hence the name.

    Radio galaxies emit radiation in the form of synchrotron emission. How do we know? Because of the intense polarization of this emission. Magnetobremsstrahlung radiation (don't worry, it is just another name for synchrotron emission) is emitted when a charged particle is accelerated in a direction perpendicular to its velocity. Such radiation carries a clear sign of polarization, so you can now understand why radiation from radio galaxies is strongly polarized.

    Synchrotron emission implies that the plasma producing these radio waves contains electrons as well as protons and positrons. The problem is that we have no way to find the particle content from the radiation itself. Synchrotron radiation is also not limited to radio wavelengths: if a radio source can accelerate particles to high enough energies, the characteristics seen at radio wavelengths may also be visible in infrared, optical, ultraviolet or even X-ray wavelengths. Other emission processes, such as inverse Compton radiation, happen along with synchrotron emission. To distinguish between them we use polarization and the continuum spectrum, but it is a challenging process.

    How to identify radio objects

    If you want to study a thing, you have to look at it. These radio objects are very far away. Consider NGC 5128: it is almost 60,000 light-years away, but the good thing is that its emission lines are strong and sharp in its spectra. I am emphasising that these sources are very far away. You need telescopes with high angular resolution and radio sensitivity, but there is a problem: for a telescope with a single antenna, increasing the angular resolution means increasing its diameter. To satisfy radio astronomers, we would require a telescope whose diameter is almost 1.5 km. The largest single-dish telescope in the world is the Five Hundred Meter Aperture Spherical Telescope in China, with a diameter of 500 metres. If you want to build one bigger than that, good luck!

    Radio astronomers found a way to achieve higher angular resolution without building giant telescopes, using three techniques: the radio interferometer, aperture synthesis, and lunar occultation.

    Radio interferometer

    Astronomers link arrays of smaller telescopes together to create a giant aperture telescope. An array of this type is called an interferometer. The distance between the telescopes, rather than the size of the individual telescopes, determines the interferometer's resolution. We can thus construct an interferometer with the resolution of a single telescope hundreds of metres across.

    Instead of collecting pictures of the target, an interferometer records the interference pattern (or interference fringes) formed when light from two or more telescopes is combined. Interference fringes form when light waves interact constructively, resulting in a pattern of light and dark bands. The amplitude of the fringes encodes information about the object's size, shape and brightness distribution.

    To produce fringes, each telescope's source light must travel the same distance, down to a fraction of a wavelength (less than 1 micron for optical and near-infrared light). The light from the radio source arrives at one telescope before it reaches another, as shown in the diagram below. We transport the light from each telescope through vacuum tubes into a laboratory to correct for the varying lengths it has travelled to reach each telescope.

    Say you have two radio antennae at a distance d from each other. The signals we receive show an interference pattern at the receiver end. The line joining the two antennae is called the baseline, and its length depends not only on the spacing of the antennae but also on their orientation. At the different baseline sizes, the receiver collects the radio output and integrates it with the help of a Fourier transform.

    Fourier alertThe Fourier transform is a mathematical function that transforms a signal from the time domain to the frequency domain. This helps you understand the frequencies present inside the signal.

    The Fourier transform is essential for an interferometer because it improves the efficiency, accuracy and repeatability of the measurement. It also gives valuable information about the diameter and physical nature of radio sources. To get a good angular resolution, we have to increase the length between the telescopes. Such high resolution is made possible by constructing a Very Long Baseline Interferometer (VLBI), which can span countries and even continents. The Jodrell Bank radio astronomy group and the National Radio Astronomy Observatory use the same technique to acquire images of radio objects.

    Aperture synthesis

    An aperture synthesis telescope consists of an array of dishes connected in pairs. As the Earth rotates, each pair traces out one ring of a much larger dish, whose diameter is equal to the maximum separation of the pairs. Some dishes may be movable to provide a broader range of separations. It is a technique for achieving excellent angular resolution in radio astronomy, simulating a single large-aperture telescope with an array of telescopes. An aperture of any size can feasibly be generated by moving the two interferometer elements to all possible points in the desired aperture.

    All synthesis telescopes take advantage of the fact that the Earth's rotation sweeps out half a ring of the synthesised aperture over 12 hours (Earth-rotation synthesis); the other half of the ring is then calculated from the first half's observations. The elements are only moved to sweep out consecutive rings after that. In practice, some aperture-synthesis telescopes use several movable dishes to reduce observing time. Examples include the Multi-Element Radio-Linked Interferometer Network (MERLIN), the Ryle Telescope, the Very Large Array, the Westerbork Synthesis Radio Telescope, and the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT) at Pune.

    Lunar occultation

    While observing astronomical objects, some unwanted things can become a problem, such as the Moon itself. The Moon is a headache for optical astronomers, but it is beneficial for radio astronomers. As the Moon travels around us, it blocks the radio waves coming from radio objects. Astronomers study the change in the intensity of the radio flux coming from the source. This occultation helps determine the location and size of the radio object.

    Astronomers can face problems while using this method:

    • Only sources that lie on the Moon's path can be studied.
    • Occultation of a particular source happens only once a year, and only for a short time.
    • The Moon also emits radio waves, much more intense than the very weak sources we are trying to study.

    Key takeaways

      What to remember

    • Radio astronomy began in the early 1930s with Karl Jansky, before World War II.
    • Discrete radio sources were shown to lie beyond the solar system, and many were identified with galaxies and supernova remnants.
    • Radio galaxies emit polarized synchrotron radiation, and emit more energy in radio than in optical wavelengths.
    • Interferometry, aperture synthesis and lunar occultation give high angular resolution without building impossibly large dishes.
