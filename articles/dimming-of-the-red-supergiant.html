The Dimming of the Red Supergiant

The star in Orion's shoulder lost three quarters of its light in four months. Was a supernova on the way?

AS
Abhisek Swain
BS-MS 2019
- min read
In this article

    A bright red star embedded in the shoulder of the constellation Orion started to dwindle in brilliance back in October 2019. Edward Guinan and Richard Wasatonic, two astrophysicists at Villanova University in Pennsylvania, and their team first noticed this abnormal dip in its brightness. Within four months the star had dimmed to one-fourth of its original brightness. By mid-February 2020 it had become the 24th brightest star in the night sky, falling 14 places from being the 10th brightest four months earlier. Betelgeuse had never fluctuated by this much in its astronomical records.

    A colossal star

    Betelgeuse is a red supergiant, a stage in the life cycle of high-mass stars between 10 and 40 solar masses. Dwarf, giant and supergiant are classes that astronomers assign to stars based on the total amount of light they emit in a fixed time interval. Dwarfs are fainter than giants, and supergiants emit more light than giants.

    Betelgeuse is colossal. It has a radius 900 times that of our Sun. Imagine that Betelgeuse and our Sun switched places, with its centre located where the Sun's centre once lay. The radius of Betelgeuse would extend beyond the orbit of Jupiter, engulfing Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter and all their moons, and roasting Saturn and its icy moons to a crisp.

    Betelgeuse is also extremely luminous. Luminosity is the intrinsic brightness of an object, independent of the distance from which it is measured. To put this in perspective, imagine two identical candles, one close to you and the other at a distance. The former will look brighter and the latter dimmer, but they still have one property that is equal: their luminosity. They produce the same amount of light. Stars appear dimmer than a torch because they are very far away, even though a star emits unfathomably more light. Thus a star is more luminous than a torch, but a torch is brighter.

    In that respect, Betelgeuse outshines the Sun by between 90,000 and 150,000 times.

    Living fast, dying young

    Massive stars live aggressively and die violently. They quickly tread through the stages of their life cycle, rapidly burning off their hydrogen fuel and fabricating heavier and heavier elements as far as their mass permits. They end their lives with a cataclysmic burst called a supernova, dispersing their chemically enriched interiors across the neighbourhood.

    The star held between 15 and 25 times the Sun's heft when it was a main-sequence star, putting it in the category of high-mass stars. Betelgeuse is a toddler among stars in cosmic terms. It is only 8 million years old and is already on the verge of ending its brief life in a supernova, in about 100,000 years. The sudden plunge in its brightness sent a wriggle of excitement through the astronomy community worldwide, hinting at the likelihood of an early supernova, possibly within our puny lifetimes. But in late February the brightness began to increase, and has been increasing since, killing the hopes of witnessing an incredibly rare spectacle.

    Measuring the dimming

    Astronomers measure brightness in units called apparent magnitude, which is reverse logarithmic. An increase in apparent magnitude corresponds to a decrease in brightness. It is analogous to the pH scale, in which a higher pH indicates a lower acidity. A difference of 5 apparent magnitudes is equivalent to a 100-fold difference in brightness. Doing the math, a one-magnitude increment means the brightness changes by a factor of 2.51.

    Why did it dim?

    There are two possible explanations. One reason for the dimming could be internal changes in the star that lead to decreased luminosity. For the simultaneous conservation of gravitational and thermodynamic energies inside the star, a decrease in size also reduces the luminosity. To interpret this physically, a star with a large surface area lets a substantial amount of light escape from its surface, while a small star with a small surface area has little room for light to escape copiously.

    Thus a decrease in luminosity corresponds to a change in the star's size. Plugging the numbers in, the star would have to have shrunk by 8% to account for the plunge in luminosity. But observations do not support this.

    Stars have convection cells that appear as dark patches on their surface, forged in a process called “dredge up”, in which hot material rises from the core and cools at the surface. On the Sun these are numerous and small, about the size of France. On a red supergiant like Betelgeuse, these cells are few and titanic, as big as the orbit of Mars. These cells, along with the rotation of the star, cause semi-regular variability in its apparent brightness. But no convection cell could explain the sheer drop that happened to Betelgeuse. The surface temperature of the star was observed to have decreased by 100 °C, from 3650 to 3550 degrees. The star cooled a bit, just not enough to be evidence of convection cells causing this.

    A more promising explanation is circumstellar dust. There could be immense blobs of gas and dust surrounding the star that simply absorb and block a significant part of the light from reaching us. These clouds of dust form when bursts of radiative pressure come from the core as it contracts. Material from inside the star bubbles up to the surface, smearing out in the vicinity of the star. Red giants lose mass by this process. This material then envelops the star, blurring the border between the surface and outer space. Betelgeuse hence doesn't have a well-defined photosphere. It is a body that gradually transitions into its outer atmosphere.

    The verdict

    Researchers Emily Levesque and Philip Massey looked at titanium oxide, which is ejected by red giants. Spectroscopy shows an abundance of absorption lines of titanium oxide in the spectrum, which is directly correlated with the surface temperature. By this method, the temperature was calculated, and it did not show a drop low enough to support the former idea. Their research paper has been accepted worldwide, and the latter explanation has been agreed upon.

    Space weather forecasts have once again pushed the date of the inevitable Betelgeuse supernova to about 100,000 years into the future. When it does happen, our descendants will be able to see it as bright as a full Moon, visible even in broad daylight. A time bomb is ticking 700 light-years away, waiting to set our night sky ablaze.

    Also published in Chrysalis.

    Key takeaways

      What to remember

    • In 2019–20, Betelgeuse dimmed to about a quarter of its brightness, to a record low of magnitude +1.61.
    • Shrinking and giant convection cells could not explain a drop that large. The leading explanation is a cloud of dust ejected by the star.
    • Betelgeuse is a red supergiant about 900 times the Sun's radius, and it will end as a supernova in around 100,000 years.
