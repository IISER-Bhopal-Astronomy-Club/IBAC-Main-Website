Lagrange Points: The Cosmic Pit-Stops

Five points where the pull of two bodies and the spin of their frame cancel out. Why spacecraft park at three of them, and asteroids gather at the other two.

SP
Shaunak Padhyegurjar
BS-MS 2019
- min read
In this article

    Lagrange points are points near two massive co-orbiting bodies that remain fixed relative to the two massive bodies, and a small mass kept at these points will experience no force. This is a consequence of the gravitational force due to the two large bodies and the centrifugal force cancelling out at these points.

    Lagrange points at a glance

    Number of points
    Five, labelled L1 to L5
    L1, L2, L3
    On the line through the two large bodies. Unstable
    L4, L5
    Vertices of equilateral triangles. Stable if M1/M2 ≥ 24.9599
    Spacecraft
    SOHO at L1. JWST and Gaia at L2
    Trojans
    Over a million at Jupiter’s L4 and L5

    Introduction

    It turns out that there are five such points, labelled L1 to L5, around any pair of massive bodies that orbit around their centre of mass. For example, the Sun–Earth system has one set of five Lagrange points, while the Earth–Moon system has a different set. The L1, L2 and L3 points lie along the line passing through the centres of the two large bodies, while the L4 and L5 points each lie at the third vertex of an equilateral triangle formed with the centres of the two large bodies. Thus all Lagrange points lie in the same 2D plane containing the two massive bodies.

    Lagrange points are named in honour of the French-Italian mathematician and physicist Joseph Louis Lagrange. Given below is an image showing the Lagrange points of the Sun–Earth system.

    The problem

    The problem of finding Lagrange points around a system of two massive bodies is actually a three-body problem. The three-body problem is not exactly solvable. However, if the mass of the third body is negligible compared to the other two masses, we can ignore the reaction force of the third mass on the other two. Hence the problem of Lagrange points is a restricted three-body problem. Leonhard Euler had discovered the existence of the Lagrange points L1, L2 and L3 a few years before Lagrange discovered the points L4 and L5. Lagrange studied the general three-body problem extensively.

    Consider two massive bodies of masses M1 and M2 (let M1 > M2) orbiting around their centre of mass (assume circular orbits). Let their positions be r1 and r2 (bold face is used to denote vectors and normal face to denote their magnitudes throughout), respectively, with the origin of the coordinate system at the centre of mass of the two bodies. Let m be a negligible mass compared to M1 and M2, located in the orbital plane of M1 and M2. Let r be its position vector. All these terms are shown in the figure below.

    Finding the positions of the Lagrange points

    Using force

    We have to find general solutions for the position of the Lagrange points. First, let's find the general equation for the total gravitational force at a point in the orbital plane where the mass m is located. From Figure 2, we obtain the total gravitational force:

    F = −(GM1m / |r − r1|3)(r − r1) − (GM2m / |r − r2|3)(r − r2)(1)

    To find the Lagrange points, one can solve equation 1 by setting F = 0. However, note that r1 and r2 are changing with time. One can obtain the solution for r1(t) and r2(t) by solving the two-body problem and then look for a solution of the equation F(t) = mr̈(t). This will keep the relative positions of the three bodies fixed. The stationary solutions obtained by this method will be the Lagrange points.

    However, the easiest method to solve for stationary solutions is to adopt a co-rotating frame of reference, in which the masses M1 and M2 are at fixed positions with their centre of mass as the origin. The co-rotating frame simulates the orbital motion of the two masses. As this frame of reference is rotating, it has some angular frequency Ω, which is the same as the angular frequency of the orbiting bodies. Thus, from Kepler's third law, we have

    Ω2R3 = G(M1 + M2)(2) · where R is the distance between the two masses

    Since we have adopted a co-rotating frame of reference, which is a non-inertial frame, we need to include pseudo-forces in the equation for the total force. Equation 1 now becomes

    Frot = −(GM1m / |r − r1|3)(r − r1) − (GM2m / |r − r2|3)(r − r2) − 2m(Ω × ṙ) − m(Ω × Ω × r)(3)

    where Ω is the angular velocity and ṙ is the velocity of the mass m. The third term in the above equation represents the Coriolis force, and the last term represents the centrifugal force. Note that the Euler force (−m Ω̇ × r) is not included, as the angular velocity Ω is assumed to be constant due to circular orbits. Equation 3 can be written in terms of acceleration as

    r̈ = −(GM1 / |r − r1|3)(r − r1) − (GM2 / |r − r2|3)(r − r2) − 2Ω × ṙ − Ω × Ω × r(4)

    From Lagrangian mechanics, we know that the generalised potential U for rotating non-inertial frames is given by

    U = −GM1 / |r − r1| − GM2 / |r − r2| − 2Ω(xẏ − yẋ) − ½Ω2(x2 + y2)(5)

    The last term is called the “centrifugal potential”, corresponding to the centrifugal force. One may say that the second-last term represents a “Coriolis potential”, but this is not quite true, as the Coriolis force is non-conservative and cannot have a potential.

    Let r1 = −r1î and r2 = r2î. Now let R = −r1 + r2, which implies R = (r1 + r2)î. Thus R denotes the distance between the two massive bodies. As the origin of the system is at the centre of mass of the two massive bodies, we have

    M1r1 + M2r2 = 0(6)

    Thus r1 = −αR and r2 = βR, where α = M2 / (M1 + M2) and β = M1 / (M1 + M2). Also note that α + β = 1. For convenience, we can write the distances between the mass m and M1 and M2 respectively as

    d1 = |r − r1| = √((x + αR)2 + y2 + z2)    and    d2 = |r − r2| = √((x − βR)2 + y2 + z2)

    To find the positions of the Lagrange points, we need to obtain solutions of Frot = 0, or equivalently r̈ = 0. We can split equation 4 into x, y and z components:

    ẍ = −GM1(x + αR) / d13 − GM2(x − βR) / d23 + 2Ωẏ + Ω2x(7)
    ÿ = −GM1y / d13 − GM2y / d23 − 2Ωẋ + Ω2y(8)
    z̈ = −GM1z / d13 − GM2z / d23(9)

    As the equilibrium points lie in the orbital plane of the two masses, these points are independent of z. However, for completeness we need to include z for the stability analysis in the next section. We solve equations 7 and 8 by setting them to zero to get x and y. This involves finding the roots of three quintic polynomials, each giving the general position of one of the Lagrange points L1, L2 and L3, which are

    L1 = (R[1 − (α/3)1/3], 0, 0)
    L2 = (R[1 + (α/3)1/3], 0, 0)
    L3 = (−R[1 + 5α/12], 0, 0)

    From these solutions, we see that L1 and L2 are equidistant from M2, on opposite sides of it. However, this is not quite true: the L2 point lies further away from M2 than L1.

    Finding solutions for L4 and L5 requires a little more effort. We need to balance forces for y ≠ 0. The centrifugal force acts radially outward and needs to be balanced with the gravitational force exerted by the two masses. In the direction perpendicular to the centrifugal force, we need to balance only the gravitational forces. This suggests that we should balance forces in directions parallel and perpendicular to r. By doing this, we obtain the general coordinates of L4 and L5, which are

    L4 = ((R/2)(M1 − M2)/(M1 + M2), (√3/2)R, 0)
    L5 = ((R/2)(M1 − M2)/(M1 + M2), −(√3/2)R, 0)

    Thus L4 is located at the third vertex of an equilateral triangle of side R, formed by the two masses, and L5 is the mirror reflection of L4 about the x-axis. Hence we have found the general coordinates of all five Lagrange points by solving the equation of force.

    Using potential

    The net potential given in equation 5, in addition to position, has a velocity-dependent term. Although it has no effect on the position of the Lagrange points, it must be taken into account when looking at their stability. As we are looking for stationary solutions, let ṙ = 0. Then the potential becomes

    U = −GM1 / |r − r1| − GM2 / |r − r2| − ½Ω2r2(10)

    Differentiating U with respect to position gives us the force, as gravitational and centrifugal forces are conservative and can be expressed as F = −∇U. Thus, if we take the gradient of the potential, we can find equilibrium points by setting ∇U = 0. However, as this is equivalent to F = 0, we find ourselves back at solving the equations of force, as done previously. So instead of finding analytical solutions, let's find numerical solutions using Wolfram Mathematica.

    First, we need to make the equation of potential dimensionless, so that it can be used in Mathematica. After non-dimensionalisation (the mathematical details have been skipped; see the note at the end of this article), the potential becomes

    U(x, y) = −β / √((x/R + 1 − β)2 + y2/R2) − (1 − β) / √((x/R − β)2 + y2/R2) − ½(x2 + y2)/R2(11)

    If we plot U(x, y) and set its gradient to zero, i.e. ∇U(x, y) = 0, then we get the positions of the five Lagrange points. For some value of β and R, the effective potential was plotted in Mathematica and its gradient was set to zero to obtain the corresponding Lagrange points. Below are the contour and 3D plots of the same.

    It is quite insightful to plot the gravitational potential and the centrifugal potential separately, as in equation 11, to get a better grasp of how these two potentials behave. Below are the surface plots for the same.

    As expected, the gravitational potential tends to zero as the distance from the two masses increases. The centrifugal potential, on the other hand, becomes more negative as we move away from the system. This is also as expected, because the centrifugal force increases away from the centre of rotation. The addition of these two potentials gives us the effective potential in the rotating frame of reference, as shown in Figure 3 (a) and (b). Thus, visualising the gravitational and centrifugal potentials separately gives us a better understanding of the effective potential.

    Stability analysis

    Now that we have established the existence of fixed points in the restricted three-body problem, we also need to determine whether they are stable. The shape of the effective potential in Figure 3 gives a rough idea of their stability, but it is not sufficient, as the potential also depends on velocity. Instead we must perform a linear stability analysis about each equilibrium point and look at small perturbations. To do so, we first separate the components dependent on velocity:

    U′ = U + 2Ω(xẏ − yẋ) = −GM1 / √((x + αR)2 + y2 + z2) − GM2 / √((x − βR)2 + y2 + z2) − ½Ω2(x2 + y2)(12)

    The components of the total force given in equations 7–9 can then be written as

    ẍ = −∂U′/∂x + 2Ωẏ,    ÿ = −∂U′/∂y − 2Ωẋ,    z̈ = −∂U′/∂z(13a, 13b, 13c)

    The Taylor series expansion of U′ (up to second order) around a Lagrange point (x0, y0, z0) is

    U′ = U′0 + ½[U′xx(x − x0)2 + U′yy(y − y0)2 + U′zz(z − z0)2] + U′xy(x − x0)(y − y0)(14)

    The general coordinates of some point very close to a Lagrange point are

    x = x0 + δx,   ẋ = δẋ
    y = y0 + δy,   ẏ = δẏ
    z = z0 + δz,   ż = δż(15, 16, 17)

    Plugging these into equation 14 gives

    U′ = U′0 + ½[U′xx(δx)2 + U′yy(δy)2 + U′zz(δz)2] + U′xyδxδy(18)

    Now, using equations 13a–c and 18, we get

    δẍ = −U′xxδx − U′xyδy + 2Ωδẏ
    δÿ = −U′yyδy − U′xyδx − 2Ωδẋ
    δz̈ = −U′zzδz(19, 20, 21)

    These are the equations of motion for small perturbations around any Lagrange point. We see from them that z is independent of x and y: more specifically, δz and δż are independent of δx, δẋ, δy and δẏ, and vice versa. So we can prove that all Lagrange points are stable in the z-direction. We have the following linearised matrix equation:

    d/dt  (δz, δż)T =

    The stability of any fixed point is determined by the nature of the eigenvalues of the evolution matrix of that point. Since the eigenvalues of the coefficient matrix in equation 22 are both always imaginary, we know from the theory of stability of fixed points that they correspond to stable fixed points. Thus we have proven our claim that all Lagrange points are stable in the z-direction.

    L1, L2 and L3 are unstable

    Just by looking at the shape of the effective potential in Figure 3 (b) around L1, L2 and L3, we can say that they are unstable points, saddle points to be exact. Indeed, from the eigenvalues of their evolution matrices using the table above, we find that one eigenvalue is real and positive and one is real and negative, indicating saddle points. Hence we conclude that L1, L2 and L3 are saddle points and thus dynamically unstable. A small departure from these points will grow exponentially, and the mass m will drift away from them. Since the eigenvalues for L3 are smaller than those for L1 and L2, an object placed at L3 will drift away much more slowly than it would if displaced by the same amount from L1 or L2.

    L4 and L5 can be stable

    Looking at the plot of the effective potential, one would think that L4 and L5 are also unstable, as they correspond to local maxima of the effective potential. Quite surprisingly, stability analysis shows that L4 and L5 are in fact stable fixed points. The eigenvalues of their evolution matrices turn out to be purely imaginary, indicating stable fixed points.

    The stability of these points is due to the effect of the Coriolis force. When the mass m departs from the point, it gains speed, and the Coriolis force (which is velocity dependent) comes into play, which makes m start orbiting the Lagrange point.

    The condition for L4 and L5This stability is valid only if M1/M2 ≥ 24.9599. This condition is necessary for the eigenvalues to be purely imaginary. If it is not satisfied, even L4 and L5 become unstable. The Sun–Earth system (MSun/MEarth ≈ 333,000) and the Earth–Moon system (MEarth/MMoon ≈ 81) therefore have stable L4 and L5 points. The same applies to all Sun–planet systems in the solar system.

    Discussion

    Lagrange points are great spots to put purpose-specific artificial satellites.

    PointStabilityUses and examples
    Sun–Earth L1UnstableUninterrupted view of the Sun (NASA's Solar and Heliospheric Observatory, SOHO), and of the sunlit hemisphere of the Earth
    Sun–Earth L2UnstableSpace-based observations: the James Webb Space Telescope and ESA's Gaia observatory
    Sun–Earth L3UnstablePopular with science fiction writers as a “counter Earth”, but it could not hold a natural object for long
    L4 and L5Stable (if M1/M2 ≥ 24.9599)Pull natural objects into orbit: Trojan asteroids

    NASA's Solar and Heliospheric Observatory (SOHO) is located at the Sun–Earth L1 point, as this point provides an uninterrupted view of the Sun. For the same reason, observations of the sunlit hemisphere of the Earth can also be made from the Sun–Earth L1 point. The Sun–Earth L2 point, lying beyond the Earth, is a good spot for making space-based observations. The soon-to-be-launched James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is going to be deployed at the Sun–Earth L2 point. The ESA Gaia space observatory is also at Sun–Earth L2.

    The Sun–Earth L3 was a popular subject of science fiction writers, who would place a “counter Earth” at this point. The Sun–Earth L3 point is unstable and could not contain a natural object, large or small, for very long. This is because the gravitational forces of other planets are stronger than that of the Earth there. For example, Venus comes within 0.3 AU of this point every 20 months.

    Missions to the Lagrange points orbit the points in Lissajous orbits or halo orbits (for L1, L2 and L3), instead of occupying the actual point. (How strange yet fascinating it is to think of an object orbiting nothing but an empty point in space!) The L1, L2 and L3 points, being dynamically unstable, require periodic orbit corrections for artificial satellites to remain in the desired orbit. Thus the duration of missions going to L1–L3 is limited by the fuel reserves of the satellite.

    The L4 and L5 points, being stable, tend to pull natural objects into orbits around them. These objects are called ‘Trojans’ or ‘Trojan asteroids’. In fact, the L4 and L5 points of the Sun–Jupiter system house more Trojans (over a million) than those of any other pair of bodies in the solar system. The region of stability around L4 and L5 is in fact large, due to the effect of the Coriolis force. Due to this, some Trojans are found to exist more than 5° off 60°, where L4 and L5 are located. The Earth–Moon L4 and L5 points are stable for millions of years, if not billions of years. This is because small perturbations by other planets in the solar system eventually make an object placed at these points drift away.

    Key takeaways

      What to remember

    • Around any two orbiting bodies there are five Lagrange points. L1, L2 and L3 lie on the line through the two bodies, and L4 and L5 form equilateral triangles with them.
    • Their positions come from the restricted three-body problem, solved most easily in a co-rotating frame, or by finding where the gradient of the effective potential is zero.
    • L1, L2 and L3 are unstable saddle points, so spacecraft there need regular corrections. L4 and L5 are stable thanks to the Coriolis force, as long as M1/M2 ≥ 24.9599.
    • Spacecraft like SOHO, JWST and Gaia use L1 and L2, while L4 and L5 collect Trojan asteroids.
