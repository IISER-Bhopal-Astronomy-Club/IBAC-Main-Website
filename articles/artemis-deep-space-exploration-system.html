Artemis Mission: Deep Space Exploration System

Four systems have to work together to put people back on the Moon. A look at the rocket, the capsule, the launch site and the lander.

HS
Himadri Sonowal
BS-MS 2020
- min read
In this article

    Artemis, as quoted in the last article, is “our generation's Apollo 8.” Its first mission is a few integrated tests away from being launched into space. A huge role in the Artemis Mission is played by its Deep Space Exploration System, which consists of some very advanced systems.

    Written before launchThis article was written ahead of the Artemis I launch, which was then planned for February 2022. The dates and plans below are those at the time of writing.

    Artemis systems at a glance

    System

    The rocket: Space Launch System

    NASA, as sources reveal, is supposed to launch its massive Space Launch System (SLS) rocket for the first time in February 2022. The rocket will be part of many Artemis missions and many other missions the future holds. It is said to be the most powerful rocket NASA has ever built and, on completion, is designed to be more flexible and evolvable, increasing the possibilities for payloads and robotic scientific missions to the Moon, Saturn and Jupiter.

    The Space Launch System is a super-heavy-lift vehicle, and the only rocket capable of sending Orion, astronauts and cargo on a single mission, which makes it the foundation for human exploration beyond Earth's orbit. Artemis will use at least one SLS each year until 2030.

    The spacecraft: Orion

    NASA's Orion is meant to take humans farther, and on a deeper exploration of space, than ever before. From transporting the crew into space, to providing emergency abort capabilities, sustaining the crew during space travel, and allowing safe re-entry from deep-space return velocities, Orion will serve as an exploration vessel for many missions to come. For its targeted lift-off in February 2022, it is already mated with the powerful SLS, now nicknamed the mega-moon rocket.

    On Artemis I, the SLS is to launch an uncrewed Orion into Earth orbit and put it on a course for a lunar distant retrograde orbit, where it will go 40,000 miles beyond the Moon and 280,000 miles from Earth before coming home. This flight test is crucial: it will exhibit the performance of the SLS on its inaugural mission and gather engineering data throughout, before Orion returns on a high-speed Earth re-entry at Mach 32, or 24,500 miles per hour. The top priority is to ensure that return, the high-speed lunar-velocity re-entry, and a very much needed test of the heat shield.

    Artemis I is an uncrewed configuration, so it has no life support systems or astronaut-supporting elements. Instead it carries data-gathering tools to verify performance and to compare estimated models with actual flight data. Orion will surpass Apollo 13's record for distance travelled from Earth in a spacecraft developed for humans, and will travel an estimated distance of more than 1.4 million miles before returning.

    The crew module for Artemis I has been tested, assembled and integrated with the European service module. The service module, built by ESA, provides most of the propulsion, power and cooling systems for the crew module, where astronauts will live and work during future Artemis missions.

    The launch team: Exploration Ground Systems

    Exploration Ground Systems (EGS) is one of three NASA programs based at NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida. EGS was established to develop and operate the systems and facilities necessary to process and launch rockets and spacecraft during assembly, transport and launch. Its mission is to transform the centre from a historically government-only launch complex to a spaceport that can handle several different kinds of spacecraft and rockets, both government and commercial.

    The Human Landing System

    The Human Landing System (HLS) is the final mode of transportation that will take astronauts to the lunar surface in the Artemis programme. On early missions, astronauts will live inside the pressurised crew cabin of the lander for up to a week. NASA experts will work closely with business partners to build their own human settlements, using decades of human spaceflight experience and commercial speed to achieve the Moon landing planned for 2024.

    The HLS programme will also carry out improved development and risk-reduction activities, and work in tandem with strategic planning and contractors for the 2024 mission, as well as with the maturity of the programmes required for the construction of sustainable future structures.

    The Lunar Gateway, though independent, is also a vital part of the Artemis Mission. A brief on the Gateway is given in the article Artemis Mission: Humans to the Moon Again.

    NASA is planning an Artemis Base Camp concept that includes a modern lunar cabin, a rover, and even a mobile home. For the first few missions, the HLS will double as lunar lodging, offering life support for a short crew stay on the Moon. In the future, NASA envisions a fixed habitat at the Artemis Base Camp that can house up to four astronauts for a month-long stay.

    Betimes to the Moon, and ere long soon to Mars and beyond.

    This is still an aspiration that would be thought of as a daydream, if these few developments did not exist.

    Key takeaways

      What to remember

    • The Space Launch System is a super-heavy-lift rocket able to send Orion, astronauts and cargo on a single mission.
    • Orion carries the crew, with a service module built by ESA. Artemis I was planned as an uncrewed test of the heat shield and re-entry.
    • Exploration Ground Systems prepares and launches the vehicles from Kennedy Space Center.
    • The Human Landing System takes astronauts to the surface and doubles as lodging for early missions.
