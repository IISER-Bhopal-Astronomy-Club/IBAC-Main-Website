Hubble Sequence of Galaxy Classification

Galaxies come in a surprising number of shapes. Here is the classic way astronomers sort them, and what the sorting tells us.

VG
Vedant Girnare
BS-MS 2022
- min read
In this article

    At large scales, the basic constituents of the universe are galaxies. There are about 1011 in the observable universe. Galaxies have a broad range of physical properties which presumably reflect their evolutionary and formative histories and give rise to different types of morphologies. Understanding galaxy formation and evolution is one of the main goals of cosmology.

    The first step in any empirical science is to look for patterns and trends. Galaxies are classified into groups based on their observable properties, such as shape, size, colour, and the presence or absence of certain features. The classification of galaxies helps astronomers to better understand the nature and evolution of these objects.

    One of the most common ways of classifying galaxies is by their morphology, or shape. The most well-known scheme was developed by the astronomer Edwin Hubble in the 1920s, and is known as the Hubble sequence or Hubble tuning-fork diagram. It divides galaxies into three main types: elliptical, spiral, and irregular. At the end, we will discuss the colour-morphology relation as an example of how the physical properties of a galaxy relate to its morphology and verify trends in the classification.

    Spiral galaxies

    Our own Milky Way is a spiral galaxy. Its spiral structure was difficult to establish observationally, since we view it from within. It is easier to see this structure in other galaxies, unless we are viewing them edge-on. Our nearest large galaxy, M31 in the Andromeda constellation, has a similar spiral structure. Spiral galaxies are the most numerous among the various types of bright galaxies. Like the Milky Way, they exhibit rotation, flattening with a central bulge, and dark lanes of absorbing matter.

    The various classes of spiral galaxies are called Sa, Sb, Sc and so on. The sequence is in decreasing order of the importance of the central nucleus or bulge in relation to the surrounding disc. Along the sequence, the central spheroid has decreasing luminosity and the spiral arms become more loosely wound. Our galaxy and M31 are of type Sb.

    In spiral galaxies such as our Milky Way, density waves are associated with the spiral arm patterns that are often observed. The arms sweep outward from the galactic centre and are made up of areas where matter is more densely concentrated (density peaks) and regions where it is less concentrated (density troughs). These waves create regions of increased density and trigger the formation of stars within the spiral arms.

    Barred spirals

    Some spirals have bars in the central region. These are called barred spirals and are categorized as SBa, SBb, and so on. Half of all disk galaxies show a central bar, which contains up to one third of the total light. Bars are a form of dynamical instability in differentially rotating stellar disks. Bar patterns are not static: they rotate with a pattern speed, but unlike spiral arms, they are not density waves. Stars in the bar stay in the bar.

    Elliptical galaxies

    While spirals are the most numerous among bright galaxies, the most numerous among all galaxies are the ellipticals. These are ellipsoidal in shape, exhibit very little rotation, and have very little gas and dust. The various types of ellipticals are placed in the sequence E0, E1, ... E7. This sequence describes progressively flattened profiles, E0 being nearly spherical and E7 of markedly flattened lenticular form. For a galaxy of type En, the number n is given by:

    n = 10 (1 − b/a) where a and b are the major and minor axes of the ellipse respectively

    Isophotes are contours of constant surface brightness. The ratio of semi-major to semi-minor axis measures how far the isophote deviates from a circle (1 − b/a). This allows us to classify elliptical galaxies by type En, where n = 10(1 − b/a).

    Unlike star images, which tend to be point-like, galaxies have nebulous shapes like those described above. Astronomers can measure the distribution of light across a galaxy with great accuracy using solid-state instruments such as the charge-coupled device (CCD), and this distribution is conveniently described in terms of isophotes. For many galaxies, especially the ellipticals, increasing the sensitivity of measurement shows that the boundary of a galaxy does not come to an abrupt end. Rather, the light intensity gradually diminishes outwards from the centre. Astronomers therefore often use the so-called Holmberg radius, which corresponds to the isophote at which the surface brightness drops to a specific value, to indicate the optical boundary of a galaxy.

    Lenticular galaxies (S0)

    Another type of galaxy, called S0, is intermediate between the ellipticals and the spirals. Like the ellipticals, S0 galaxies have little gas and dust, but their isophotes are more like those of the spirals. These galaxies may have formed from collisions of ellipticals and spirals. Collisions of galaxies are not uncommon, especially in rich clusters. Stars may go through a collision relatively unscathed since they are widely spaced, but interstellar gas and dust may be spewed out into intergalactic space. In such a case, the isophotes may remain intact.

    S0 galaxies have a rotating disk in addition to a central elliptical bulge, but the disk lacks spiral arms or prominent dust lanes, meaning no active star formation. Lenticulars can also have a central bar, in which case they are labelled SB0.

    Irregular galaxies

    Lastly, there are galaxies broadly classified as irregular. As the name implies, these have no set structural pattern. There are three major types: Irr I, Irr II, and dIrr.

    • An Irr I galaxy features some structure, but not enough to place it cleanly into the Hubble sequence. Subtypes with some spiral structure are called Sm galaxies, and those without spiral structure are called Im galaxies.
    • An Irr II galaxy does not appear to feature any structure that can place it into the Hubble sequence.
    • A dIrr is a dwarf irregular galaxy.

    Dwarf irregulars are now thought to be important for understanding the overall evolution of galaxies, as they tend to have a low level of metallicity and relatively high levels of gas, and are thought to be similar to the earliest galaxies that populated the Universe. They may represent a local (and therefore more recent) version of the faint blue galaxies known to exist in deep-field galaxy surveys. Some irregular galaxies, especially of the Magellanic type, are small spiral galaxies that are being distorted by the gravity of a larger neighbour.

    It is hoped that when the structure and evolution of galaxies is better understood, such classifications can be better appreciated. Meanwhile, they may be regarded as empirical indicators of galactic physics, just as the empirical classification of stars helped bring about the eventual understanding of stellar structure and evolution.

    What changes along the sequence

    The Hubble sequence turned out to be surprisingly robust: many, but not all, physical properties of galaxies correlate with the morphological classification.

    PropertyEllipticals (E, S0)Irregulars (Irr)
    SupportPressure supportRotational support
    Star formationPassiveActively star forming
    ColourRedBlue
    GasHot gasCold gas and dust
    StarsOldStill forming
    Luminosity densityHighLow

    Properties change steadily through E, S0, Sa, Sb, Sc and Irr. But masses, luminosities, sizes and similar fundamental properties do not correlate well with the Hubble type: at every type there is a large spread.

    A closer look: colour and morphology

    Let's look at one of these trends in more detail. A classic study was done by Holmberg (1958), who compiled and analysed photometric data (integrated magnitudes, colours and diameters) for 300 galaxies. One of his main conclusions was the dependence of colour on morphological type. Objects of different morphological classes show clear differences in their optical colours, as measured by the colour indices (U−B) and (B−V). The figure below shows the well-established trend between morphology and mean colour. The E and S0 galaxies are clearly redder than their spiral counterparts, and the trend from redder to bluer is nearly monotonic.

    At the same time, the range of colours among Sa galaxies overlaps that of Sc galaxies: some Sc's are as red as some Sa's, while some Sa's are as blue as some Sc's. It is unlikely that this overlap results from misclassification or observational errors. Rather, the scatter reflects true variations in the colours, and presumably the current star formation rates, of individual objects.

    Elliptical (E) and lenticular (S0) galaxies are redder than spirals. This suggests differences in stellar populations and star formation histories. The redder colour of ellipticals and lenticulars can be attributed to older stellar populations and a lack of ongoing star formation.

    Spiral galaxies typically form through more orderly and less violent processes than ellipticals. They often originate from the collapse of interstellar gas and dust clouds, which can lead to a rotating disk-like structure. This disk is conducive to ongoing star formation because gas and dust settle into the plane of the galaxy and form new stars over time. Along the Sa, Sb, Sc sequence, the spiral arms become more loosely wound and much more significant. Since it is the disk and spirals that favour star formation, younger and still-forming stars are more present in Sc than Sa, and hence we see the trend.

    In contrast, elliptical and lenticular galaxies are believed to have formed through more violent processes, such as mergers and interactions, which can redistribute stars and remove angular momentum. These processes often lead to a random, spheroidal distribution of stars. They can also disrupt the gas and dust needed for ongoing star formation. As a result, star formation in these galaxies is often quenched or significantly reduced after their initial formation, leading to older stellar populations. And since lenticulars lack spiral arms, no active star formation takes place in them.

    How, then, do we explain the overlap in colours between Sa and Sc? It suggests that the colour-morphology relationship is not entirely deterministic and can vary within these subcategories. The colour differences among galaxy types, and the scatter within specific subcategories, suggest that galaxies do not evolve along a strictly defined path. Their evolution is influenced by a variety of factors, including interactions with other galaxies, internal processes, and the availability of gas and dust for new star formation.

    Overall, Holmberg's study provides valuable insight into the relationship between galaxy morphology and optical colour. It also emphasizes the need for a more nuanced understanding of galaxy evolution that considers the range of factors influencing galaxy properties and appearances.

    Conclusion

    While the Hubble classification is a valuable tool for understanding and organizing galaxies, it may not always be a complete predictor of all galaxy properties or behaviours. To gain a more comprehensive understanding, researchers often combine morphological classifications with additional information such as spectral data, which provides insight into the composition and physical processes within galaxies.

    The Hubble sequence, proposed by Edwin Hubble in the 1920s, has proven to be an indispensable framework for categorizing the staggering diversity of galaxies. By classifying galaxies into distinct types based on their visual appearance and structure, such as ellipticals, lenticulars and spirals, with further subdivisions, it offers a systematic approach that has facilitated astronomical research for generations. It has been instrumental in identifying broad trends in galaxy properties, such as the correlation between morphology and star formation, and has provided a foundation for studying the evolution, dynamics and interactions of galaxies.

    Key takeaways

      What to remember

    • The Hubble sequence sorts galaxies by shape into ellipticals, lenticulars (S0), spirals (including barred spirals) and irregulars.
    • Many properties change smoothly along the sequence, including colour, star formation, and the type of gas.
    • Mass, luminosity and size do not follow the sequence well, and colours overlap between neighbouring types.
    • Morphology is best used together with other data, such as spectra.
