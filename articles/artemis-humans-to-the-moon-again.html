Artemis Mission: Humans to the Moon Again

Half a century after Apollo, NASA plans to go back. A guide to the three Artemis missions and the Gateway station.

HS
Himadri Sonowal
BS-MS 2020
- min read
In this article

    It has been half a century since humans last stepped on the Moon during the Apollo programme. Now NASA is planning to take this a step further with the implementation of Apollo's twin sister, the Artemis Mission.

    Written before launchThis article was written before Artemis I flew, when a crewed mission was planned for 2023 and a landing for 2024. The schedule below is the plan at the time of writing.

    Artemis at a glance

    Introduction

    As NASA contemplated the idea of humans in space, the robotic expeditions of deep space exploration saw a plethora of technological advancements and scientific breakthroughs. Although living on the International Space Station has its own appeal, the allure of venturing further into the solar system is more incredible. With this mission, NASA aims to put the first woman and the next man on the lunar surface by 2024. Furthermore, it aspires to extend the human voyage to Mars sooner, which indicates that the mission runs two-fold.

    NASA's Deep Space Exploration System

    The Artemis mission serves as a practice run for NASA to test its new Deep Space Exploration System, which includes four parts:

    1. The spacecraft: Orion
    2. The rocket: Space Launch System
    3. The launch team: Exploration Ground Systems
    4. The Human Landing System

    This system will make Artemis I the first-ever test of an integrated deep space exploration system. The Orion spacecraft, powered by an ESA (European Space Agency) service module, was built particularly for deep space human operations for up to four crew members. The SLS rocket is the human-rated heavy-lift rocket that will launch Orion on many missions to the Moon. At Kennedy, the exploration grounds are constantly modernising their spaceports. Meanwhile, the Human Landing System is the ultimate method of transportation that will deliver people to the lunar surface. On early flights, astronauts will spend up to a week inside the pressurised crew cabin section of the lander. A series of missions will take place before humans are finally sent to the Moon.

    Each of these systems is covered in detail in the follow-up article, Artemis Mission: Deep Space Exploration System.

    The Artemis missions

    The Artemis Mission will live through a series of missions that, quite literally, started on their track this year. It wouldn't be a stretch to say it commands half a century of preparation and experience to establish a human-robot kinship around the Moon.

    Artemis I

    As mentioned, Artemis I will be the first integrated test in a series of progressively more complicated missions that will serve as a basis for human deep space exploration, and demonstrate our dedication and competence to extend human existence to the Moon and beyond.

    As a NASA article puts it: during this flight, the spacecraft will launch on the most powerful rocket in the world and fly farther than any spacecraft built for humans has ever flown. It will travel 280,000 miles from Earth, thousands of miles beyond the Moon, over the course of about a three-week mission. Orion will stay in space longer than any ship for astronauts has done without docking to a space station, and return home faster and hotter than ever before.

    As the spacecraft makes an orbit of Earth, it will deploy its solar arrays, and the Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) will give Orion the push it needs to leave Earth's orbit for the Moon. From there, Orion will part ways with the ICPS, which deploys a number of small satellites known as CubeSats. These will perform experiments on their own within about two hours after launch.

    Artemis II

    The crewed explorations of the mission will commence in 2023 with the SLS and Orion, with four astronauts on board. “We are the Artemis Generation,” as Jim Bridenstine noted, and this will be the “Apollo 8” moment of our generation. This mission will serve as the perfect demonstration of what to expect from Artemis III, when humans will finally walk on the surface. It will provide the performance data and operational experience that the crew will acquire aboard Orion on their adventure, which is estimated at 10 days.

    The Artemis II crew will travel 4,600 miles (7,400 km) beyond the far side of the Moon. From this vantage point, they will be able to see the Earth and the Moon from Orion's windows, with the Moon close in the foreground and the Earth about a quarter-million miles in the background.

    Artemis III

    Artemis III is the apotheosis of all the missions and tests, after which the most awaited spacewalk will follow, when the crew will fly again in the Orion capsule, adding another chapter to NASA's unmatched history. This is the mission in which NASA will land the first woman and the next man on the Moon.

    Gateway

    The Lunar Gateway is essentially a tiny space station in lunar orbit. It serves as a multi-purpose outpost that provides basic life support for astronauts after they arrive in Orion to visit the lunar surface, and supports other deep space exploration.

    Gateway is an elemental part of the Artemis Mission as well as of the Deep Space Exploration System, but it was decided to keep it independent of the Artemis mission. Although future Artemis missions will certainly use the Gateway, the earlier missions were exempted.

    All the components needed to make this mission a success, and to send the next humans to walk on the Moon, are whole but en route.

    Betimes to the Moon, and ere long soon to Mars and beyond.

    Key takeaways

      What to remember

    • Artemis is NASA's programme to return humans to the Moon, and to prepare for Mars.
    • It tests a Deep Space Exploration System of Orion, the SLS rocket, Exploration Ground Systems and a Human Landing System.
    • Artemis I is the uncrewed test, Artemis II is a crewed flyby, and Artemis III is the landing.
    • The Lunar Gateway is a small space station in lunar orbit, independent of the early missions.
